Why does it rotate?

For an on-axis image point at zᵢ, a ray leaving aperture coordinate (xₐ, yₐ) at z = 0 reaches

1/f = 1/dₒ + 1/dᵢ; m = −dᵢ/dₒ
r(z) = (1 − t) a + t m u; t = z/dᵢ

Here a is the pupil coordinate and u is the physical source coordinate. At focus r = m u: extended sources form an inverted image. For a point source (u = 0), before focus the aperture scale is positive. After focus both coordinates change sign: a 180° rotation, rather than a single-axis mirror reflection.

What this model assumes

An ideal, aberration-free thin lens with the aperture at the lens and uniform pupil ray density. The image distance and magnification follow the thin-lens equation. At dₒ = 2f, dᵢ = 2f and m = −1; at dₒ = 5f, dᵢ = 1.25f and m = −0.25. Source size scales its transverse coordinates; a point source remains dimensionless.

Extended sources are spatially incoherent and uniformly bright inside their shapes, retaining source RGB colors through defocus. The display adds ray contributions from source points, with brightness normalized in each frame. The image is inverted; its magnification changes with object distance. A one-pixel reconstruction smooths discrete ray sampling. Diffraction, interference and aberrations are omitted. At exact focus the geometric footprint is a point; the visible dot is a display marker. A real optical system has a finite diffraction pattern.

Try the arrow or F first. A circle or square hides the 180° rotation because of its symmetry. All controls run locally, with no external dependencies.
